Fairshake backs 32 House candidates as crypto bill stalls

Fairshake named 32 House candidates for midterm support, including six $1 million commitments, as its preferred market-structure bill remains stalled in the Senate.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomOct 6, 2026 · 02:55 UTC#177c243 min read

Fairshake has named 32 House candidates for midterm support as its preferred crypto market-structure bill remains stalled in the Senate, pairing six $1 million commitments with a larger bipartisan slate. The group’s House push is broad, but smaller than its planned nearly $30 million against Ohio Democrat Sherrod Brown, a Senate candidate it targeted after Democrats blocked the legislation. The Associated Press report on Fairshake’s new slate says the list includes 19 Republicans and 13 Democrats.

Who gets the largest commitments, and what remains undisclosed?

Three Republicans and three Democrats are each set to receive $1 million in support, according to AP. The Republicans are House Financial Services Committee Chairman French Hill of Arkansas, subcommittee Chairman Bryan Steil of Wisconsin and committee Vice Chair Bill Huizenga of Michigan. The Democrats are Janelle Bynum of Oregon, Steven Horsford of Nevada and Derek Tran of California.

Fairshake has not said how much it will spend on the other 26 candidates. The slate also includes prominent House members from both parties, among them Democratic Caucus Chair Pete Aguilar and Republican Whip Tom Emmer. Roll Call’s account of the 32-candidate spending plan says all six recipients of the largest commitments backed the market-structure bill, as did the Democrats on the list when the measure passed the House.

The scale is therefore not yet fully visible: the six announced commitments total $6 million, while the overall spending could be higher. Fairshake had $108 million on hand at the end of August, according to Federal Election Commission figures cited by Roll Call. That balance gives the group room to expand its campaign, but does not establish how much it will ultimately spend on these races.

How does the House slate compare with Fairshake’s Senate strategy?

The House plan spreads support across party lines; the announced Senate move against Brown is a much larger, single-candidate intervention. Fairshake pledged nearly $30 million against Brown after Senate Democrats helped block the Clarity Act in September. The House slate also differs from Fairshake’s earlier activity this year: a spokesperson told AP that the group and its affiliates spent $40 million supporting 29 Democrats and $31 million supporting 28 Republicans in primaries and special elections.

That mix fits Fairshake’s stated focus on backing candidates who support crypto policy in either party. It also carries a trade-off. Funding allies on both sides may help preserve a pro-crypto bloc regardless of which party controls the House, while the Brown campaign directs far more money at a perceived opponent. The group says it won 53 of the 57 races it entered this cycle; that is its account of those contests, not a measure of whether its spending caused the outcomes.

What stalled the bill, and what could change after the midterms?

The House passed the Clarity Act in July 2025, but the Senate did not advance it after lawmakers failed to agree on ethics safeguards, among other provisions. Democrats sought stronger limits addressing President Donald Trump’s crypto interests; the Senate’s September procedural vote blocked the bill. Industry supporters say a federal framework would give crypto firms regulatory certainty, while opponents argued the measure did not adequately address conflicts of interest.

Fairshake’s political spending can influence who reaches the next Congress, but it does not resolve those policy disagreements or guarantee the legislation’s return. The next signals are the amounts actually deployed in the 32 House races, the election results, and whether lawmakers revive market-structure negotiations after November with a different balance of power or new ethics terms.
