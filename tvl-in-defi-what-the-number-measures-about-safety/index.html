TVL in DeFi: What the Number Measures About Safety

What is TVL in DeFi and does it matter for safety? It measures deposited value, not code or control risk; use it with audits, oracle and admin checks.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomNov 28, 2026 · 00:51 UTC#6d96435 min read

TVL in DeFi helps you compare how much value a protocol holds, but judging a deposit also means checking the code, oracles and people who can change its rules. A large figure can indicate that many users have committed funds; it can also mean a larger prize for an attacker. Treat TVL as context for your decision, then examine the risks that determine whether your deposit can be lost.

What is TVL in DeFi?

TVL, or total value locked, is the estimated US dollar value of assets held in a protocol’s contracts. It is a measure of deposited value, not a measure of security or the value of a protocol’s own token.

Because the assets are priced in dollars, TVL can rise or fall even when nobody deposits or withdraws. If the price of ETH rises, for instance, the dollar value of ETH held in a lending market rises too. A chart’s increase may therefore reflect token prices, new deposits, or both.

For a practical example, Morpho vaults are curated, single-asset deposit pools that allocate funds across lending markets and other supported yield sources. Background on how those vaults allocate deposits can help explain what sits behind a vault’s TVL: the figure gives its size, while its markets show where the assets are deployed.

Does a high TVL mean a DeFi protocol is safe?

No. High TVL means a protocol holds substantial value; it does not tell you whether its contracts, price oracles or administrative controls are sound. Euler Finance lost about $197 million in a March 2023 exploit despite having substantial value in its lending markets. The rekt.news leaderboard records Euler among the largest DeFi losses.

A large deposit base can reflect years of use and user confidence, but it can also make a protocol a more attractive target. The trade-off is straightforward: scale can indicate adoption and liquidity, while concentrating more funds behind the same code can increase the potential loss from a failure.

TVL is still useful for comparing protocols or tracking a change over time. Read it alongside the history behind the number: whether growth came from deposits, rising token prices or short-lived reward campaigns. That context helps you judge adoption without treating popularity as a security review.

Why can TVL overstate how much value is deposited?

TVL can count the same underlying dollars more than once when a token representing a deposit is deposited again elsewhere. Consider staked ETH: a user may stake ETH, receive a liquid staking token, then use that token as collateral in a lending market. The underlying ETH and the token representing it can appear in separate protocol totals, even though they trace back to the same capital.

Rewards can also attract deposits that leave when incentives end. A sudden rise may show that users are responding to a temporary yield, not that they intend to stay through market stress. That may still provide useful liquidity while rewards last, but the headline number gives no account of how quickly it could leave.

DefiLlama’s default TVL excludes borrowed amounts and avoids double-counting within its methodology. Its dashboard has toggles for items such as borrows, staking and double-counted deposits, so you can compare the default figure with versions that include them. When checking a protocol that accepts receipt or liquid staking tokens, compare those settings: the difference can reveal whether a headline total includes the same economic value at multiple stages.

What is the difference between TVL and market cap?

TVL measures the dollar value of assets held in a protocol’s contracts; market cap estimates the market value of a token’s circulating supply. One is a measure of funds deposited into a set of contracts, and the other is a measure of a token’s value in the market.

A protocol can have high TVL and a relatively small token market cap, or a valuable token and little deposited value. Neither number on its own tells you whether the protocol can withstand an exploit, a bad oracle price or a governance decision. Use the metric that fits your question: TVL for deposited scale, market cap for token valuation.

Where can I check a protocol’s TVL?

DefiLlama is one place to compare protocol TVL and inspect how its displayed total changes with methodology settings. Check whether the protocol page separates products or chains, and make sure you are comparing the same categories and toggles. A single combined figure may hide differences between markets with different collateral, oracles and risks.

Before depositing, review audits and their scope, how long the specific contracts have been live, the oracle design, and whether admins can change parameters or move funds. Look for timelocks that give users time to react to important changes, and check the protocol’s history of bad debt or incidents. An audit is evidence that code was reviewed; its date, coverage and unresolved findings matter.

For a constructed example, suppose you are choosing between two lending markets with similar TVL. One offers extra rewards, while the other has a longer operating history and clearly documented oracle and admin controls. Comparing the reward with those specific risks helps you decide whether the extra yield is worth the trade-off. Likewise, a large Morpho vault can lend against risky collateral: inspect its individual markets and their terms, not only the vault’s size.

Use TVL to understand how much value is deposited, then make your deposit decision from the markets, code history, oracle design and control permissions behind it.
