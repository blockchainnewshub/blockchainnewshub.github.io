Fairshake backs 32 House candidates after CLARITY Act stalls

Fairshake named 32 House candidates for midterm support, including six set for $1 million each, as the stalled CLARITY Act shifts attention to November.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomOct 8, 2026 · 21:54 UTC#1770654 min read

Fairshake has named 32 House candidates for support in the November midterms, shifting attention to a bipartisan slate after the Senate blocked the crypto industry’s priority market-structure bill. The list includes 19 Republicans and 13 Democrats, but the spending disclosed so far is concentrated: six members are set to receive $1 million in support each, while Fairshake has not specified amounts for the other 26. The Associated Press report on Fairshake’s House slate says all 32 supported the legislation.

How much is Fairshake committing to the House races?

Fairshake has identified $6 million in support for six lawmakers, but has not disclosed the total it plans to spend across the 32 races. The six are three Republicans—French Hill of Arkansas, Bryan Steil of Wisconsin and Bill Huizenga of Michigan—and three Democrats: Janelle Bynum of Oregon, Steven Horsford of Nevada and Derek Tran of California. The remaining 26 are also on the slate, but their allocations are unknown.

That leaves the announcement’s scale open. Fairshake and its affiliated PACs had about $120 million in cash at the end of August, according to AP; Roll Call reported that Fairshake alone had $108 million. Those figures describe different groupings, and neither establishes how much of the available money will go to these House races. Roll Call’s account of the PAC’s candidate list and spending says spokesperson Geoff Vetter did not disclose the amounts beyond the six $1 million commitments.

Why focus on House members after the Senate vote?

The slate backs lawmakers who supported the CLARITY Act, which passed the House in July 2025 but stalled in the Senate. The bill sought a federal framework for digital-asset markets, including rules for operations and oversight. The Senate blocked it in September after lawmakers failed to agree on ethics safeguards, among other issues; Democrats said the proposal did not adequately address President Donald Trump’s crypto interests.

That puts Fairshake’s House spending in a different posture from its most prominent Senate move. In September, the group pledged nearly $30 million against Democratic Ohio Senate candidate Sherrod Brown, while the new House slate includes members of both parties. The contrast is also visible against the 2024 cycle: crypto groups spent nearly $40 million in opposition to Brown then, and Fairshake’s network supported Democratic Senate candidates Ruben Gallego and Elissa Slotkin with about $10 million each. The House plan is bipartisan in its candidate list, but the disclosed amounts do not yet show how evenly support will be distributed across the 32 races.

What could the spending influence—and what remains unknown?

Outside spending can boost candidates in competitive reelection contests, AP reported, while the slate also gives Fairshake a route to back members who could shape a future attempt at market-structure legislation. The trade-off is that election support can preserve allies without resolving the policy disagreements that stopped the bill. Senate Democrats and Republicans failed to settle ethics provisions, and the next Congress may face changed party control.

Fairshake describes itself as issue-focused and says it backs pro-crypto candidates in both parties. It also says it and affiliated groups have won 53 of 57 races in which they engaged this cycle. Those are the group’s own measures of its record; the next test is whether the House slate translates into election results and a stronger coalition for legislation.

The immediate signals are the undisclosed spending across the other 26 House races, whether Fairshake directs more of its reported cash toward Senate contests, and which party controls Congress after November. The group has not said whether it plans further House or Senate spending before the election. What it does next will show whether the House slate is a defined allocation or an opening round in a broader campaign to shape the next Congress.
