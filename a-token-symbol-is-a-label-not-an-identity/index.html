A Token Symbol Is a Label, Not an Identity

A token symbol is a label, not an identity; network and contract address reveal whether lookalikes are the same asset, a bridged copy or unrelated tokens on different chains.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomOct 3, 2026#ca87922 min read

Two tokens can share a symbol because a symbol is a label, while a token’s contract address on a specific network identifies the token itself. Unlike a ticker on a conventional exchange, crypto symbols are not reserved in one global registry, so unrelated projects can choose the same letters.

What identifies a token if its symbol does not?

The network and contract address identify a token more reliably than its name or symbol. A token contract defines how that asset behaves on its network; another contract can use the same symbol while representing a different asset with different rules, owners or supply.

Bridges add another layer: they can create a representation of an asset on a destination network, but the relationship depends on the bridge and the contracts involved. For more on those cross-chain mechanics, see this fuller account of Polygon Bridge’s token representations across networks. A matching symbol alone cannot show whether two tokens are linked by a bridge, backed by the same asset or unrelated.

Why do wallets and exchanges show duplicate symbols?

Wallets and exchanges use symbols to make long contract addresses easier to scan, but they may choose different rules for which token to show first. Some prioritize a familiar asset or a token already in your portfolio; others display a network label or ask you to select a contract. The convenience comes at a cost: a short label hides the identifier that distinguishes lookalikes.

That difference matters most when searching, trading or sending. A search result with the expected ticker may still point to an imitation token, a bridged version or an asset on another network. A service’s listing or display choice is useful context, but it does not change the token’s contract identity.

How can you check which token you have?

Before interacting with a token, match its network and contract address against a reliable source for the asset, such as the project’s own published information. For a bridged token, check which bridge and destination contract create the representation; do not infer backing or redemption rights from the ticker.

  • Confirm the network before comparing addresses; identical-looking address strings on different networks can refer to different contracts.
  • Compare the full contract address, not just the token name, symbol or logo.
  • For a bridged asset, verify the bridge and destination token contract, and understand what claim or backing the bridge provides.
  • When a wallet or exchange offers several matches, pause and verify the intended contract before trading or sending.

The practical rule is simple: use symbols to recognize candidates, then use the network and contract to identify the token. The signals to watch are whether services display those identifiers clearly, how bridges describe the assets they issue, and whether a token’s official information matches the contract you are about to use.
