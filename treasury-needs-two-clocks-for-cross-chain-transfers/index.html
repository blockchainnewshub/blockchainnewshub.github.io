Treasury Needs Two Clocks for Cross-Chain Transfers

Cross-chain timing depends on the route and on what counts as completion: a destination balance can arrive before the source transfer is fully settled.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomOct 16, 2026 · 00:01 UTC#491cf25 min read

A treasury should allow for both the time until funds are usable on the destination chain and the longer time until the transfer is settled under the route’s rules. That is a bigger planning window than the seconds or minutes a fast route may advertise, but often shorter than waiting for a slow canonical bridge to complete every step before funds become available. The right allowance depends on the route, the amount, and whether the payment can tolerate a delay.

A cross-chain transfer is not one event. The source chain must record the deposit; a bridge or relayer must recognize it; the destination chain must record the funds; and, in some designs, the protocol later reconciles or reimburses the liquidity provider. For the treasury, the key question is which of these events releases the funds for their intended use. A balance appearing in a wallet can be enough to pay an invoice, but it may not mean every settlement step has finished.

How long does a cross-chain transfer take?

A transfer takes as long as its slowest required step before the recipient can use the funds. On a route that uses a relayer, the relayer may advance liquidity on the destination chain after seeing the source deposit, while the protocol handles reimbursement later. That can make the user-visible transfer much faster than a process that waits for finality and then completes the destination transaction.

The trade-off is between speed and what the route assumes. A relayer advances capital and accepts some exposure to the source transaction being reversed or delayed; a slower route can wait for stronger confirmation before acting. Subsequent settlement may still be needed even after the recipient sees the tokens. For example, Across describes relayers filling transfers before later reimbursement and settlement. There is no useful universal number to apply to every transfer. Source and destination chains have different block production and confirmation behavior, and congestion or a stalled transaction can extend the wait. The bridge may also impose its own verification or settlement steps. Treat a quoted estimate as an expected time for a particular route and state, not a service guarantee or a deadline suitable for every payment.

What should treasury count as completion?

Treasury should define completion by the job the funds must do, then track the transfer through the relevant status. If the purpose is a same-day payment, destination-chain availability may be the operational milestone. If the transfer is part of a reconciliation, collateral, or accounting process, final settlement may be the milestone instead.

These states are worth distinguishing in payment procedures:

  • Submitted: the source transaction has been sent, but may not yet be included in a block.
  • Confirmed: the source chain has recorded it to the required depth or finality standard.
  • Filled or received: funds have arrived on the destination chain and can be used, subject to the recipient’s own controls.
  • Settled: the bridge’s later accounting, reimbursement, or message-finalization step is complete, where the route has one.

Those labels vary by provider, so map the provider’s status messages to the treasury’s own approval and reconciliation rules. Do not mark a payment fully settled just because a wallet balance changed; equally, do not hold usable funds until a later protocol step if the payment policy only requires destination receipt.

How much buffer should a treasury add?

Build the allowance from the route’s observed or published timing for the required milestone, then add a buffer for normal variation and an exception path for delays. Avoid one blanket rule for all chains and transfer sizes. A route that fills quickly but settles later needs separate expectations for use and reconciliation; a route that waits for confirmations before releasing funds needs a buffer around that wait and the destination transaction.

For routine flows, record the route, token, source and destination networks, expected destination-availability window, and the point at which an operator should investigate. For a new route or an unusually large transfer, test the end-to-end process with a modest amount first and confirm that the recipient network and token match the payment instruction. A small test costs time and fees, but can expose an address, network, or process mismatch before it affects the main transfer.

Keep a separate contingency for a payment whose deadline cannot move. That may mean initiating earlier, holding working funds on the destination chain, or choosing a route with a more suitable completion model. Each choice has a cost: early funding ties up liquidity, while a faster route may depend on a relayer or other bridge-specific assumptions. The better policy is the one that meets the payment deadline without treating the fastest advertised path as risk-free.

What should treasury watch next?

Watch the status transitions that matter to your workflow, not only the headline estimate. Compare the time from source submission to destination availability with the time from availability to final settlement. A growing gap between those clocks may not stop a payment, but it can affect reconciliation, capital planning, and the amount of funds treasury considers in flight.

Track route changes, congestion, failed or delayed transfers, and whether providers alter their confirmation or settlement process. Revisit the buffer when those signals change or when the payment’s deadline or risk tolerance changes. The practical rule is simple: allow time to reach the state your operation actually needs, and maintain a distinct expectation for any settlement that follows.
