US Secures Court Authority Over $1.2B in Binance Hack Crypto

US authorities secured court approval to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in Binance hack proceeds; recovery now depends on tracing and forfeiture.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomNov 13, 2026 · 11:54 UTC#c3e8cd3 min read

US authorities have obtained court approval to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, moving a large share of the exchange’s $6 billion-plus loss from private-sector freezes toward government control. The Justice Department said its November 12 action is part of a broader effort that has frozen more than $2.7 billion of the stolen assets so far; the remaining work is to trace and forfeit funds still moving through crypto networks.

The distinction matters: the department described the court-authorized amount as “seize or restrain,” while the total frozen includes joint US government and private-sector efforts. The action therefore does not mean that $1.2 billion has already been returned to Binance or its users. The Associated Press reported that, in such cases, the government typically files a forfeiture claim with a judge, who determines whether anyone else has a legitimate claim to the recovered property.

From the October theft to court-authorized restraint

The FBI and Justice Department place the theft on or about October 29. The Justice Department says members of the North Korean military, whom the FBI calls TraderTraitor, stole more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance. The FBI publicly attributed the attack to North Korea on October 31. Binance had initially described a potential security issue and said its investigation suggested a compromised DevOps engineer’s laptop may have enabled unauthorized withdrawal transactions.

That sequence shows how the response has widened. Binance’s first public account concerned a suspected access route and an ongoing investigation. The FBI’s attribution named a suspected actor, while the Justice Department’s latest announcement adds a court-backed step to limit control of proceeds. Authorities and private companies had already frozen more than $2.7 billion; the new figure is the portion for which the department says it obtained court authorization to seize or restrain.

Those measures offer a way to interrupt transfers without waiting for every stolen asset to be recovered. But tracing and control remain difficult when funds cross services and networks. The Justice Department says TraderTraitor actors continue to launder assets through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges, and that efforts to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit them are ongoing. That means a freeze is a constraint on movement, not by itself proof of final recovery or ownership.

What remains unresolved

The announcement does not identify the assets covered by the court-authorized amount, explain how much is already in government-controlled wallets, or say when any forfeiture claims will be resolved. Nor does it establish that the full $2.7 billion frozen is available for return: the department’s figure combines government and private-sector action, and the legal process still has to determine claims to specific property.

The trade-off is clear. Coordinated freezes and court orders can narrow the routes available to the alleged thieves, while investigations and forfeiture proceedings take time and require authorities to establish what assets can legally be taken. The next signals are whether the government files forfeiture claims, whether more of the frozen total is brought under court authority, and whether investigators report further movement through bridges, mixers or exchanges. For now, the confirmed shift is from a broad freeze tally to a specific court-authorized action; the final recovery remains unsettled.
