Why DeFi Loans Need a Buffer Before Liquidation

Why do DeFi loans need more collateral than the loan amount? Volatile collateral needs a buffer so it can be sold before a price drop leaves debt uncovered.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomNov 27, 2026 · 07:30 UTC#0b3abe4 min read

After you supply crypto and borrow DAI, you have to lock up more value than you borrow because a DeFi protocol cannot check your credit or pursue you for repayment; the collateral has to cover the debt even as prices move. SparkLend is a useful example: its per-asset Loan-to-value (LTV) and Liquidation threshold show how that buffer is set. The trade-off is simple: you keep access to a loan without a bank’s credit assessment, but your crypto is exposed to liquidation if its value falls too far.

What replaces a credit check in a DeFi loan?

In a DeFi loan, the collateral is the repayment source. A bank can assess income and credit history, and may have legal routes to recover unpaid debt. A permissionless lending protocol cannot rely on those checks or remedies in the same way. Instead, it requires you to supply assets that can be used to cover what you borrow.

That changes how risk is measured. A bank considers the borrower; a protocol evaluates the assets in the position. SparkLend, like other money markets, lets users supply listed assets and borrow other listed assets against them. The amount you can borrow depends on the collateral asset’s LTV. Its volatility and the risk that its value could fall help explain why one asset may support a different borrowing amount from another.

LTV is the maximum borrowing value relative to the collateral’s value. It is a ceiling, not a promise that borrowing up to that amount will remain safe. The Liquidation threshold is higher than the LTV, leaving a buffer between the maximum borrow level and the point where liquidation can begin. That space gives the protocol room to respond as collateral prices change.

How does a price drop shrink the buffer?

A falling collateral price reduces how much the supplied crypto is worth while the debt remains to be repaid. Take an illustrative position: you supply $10,000 of wstETH at an 80% LTV and borrow $8,000 DAI. At the maximum LTV, the value of your debt is already 80% of the collateral’s starting value.

Now suppose wstETH falls in value. Your debt does not automatically fall with it, but your collateral is worth less. The distance between the debt and the Liquidation threshold narrows. If the position reaches that threshold, collateral can be liquidated to cover the debt. The exact point depends on the asset’s threshold and the position’s current values.

The example shows why “more collateral than I borrow” is not spare money sitting untouched. It is protection against a mismatch: crypto prices can move quickly, while the debt still needs to be repaid. A buffer can absorb some decline, but it cannot make a volatile asset stable or guarantee that liquidation will be avoided.

In practice, the figures to understand are the collateral’s current value, the amount borrowed, its LTV and its Liquidation threshold. Together, these show how much room a position has before liquidation risk rises. Borrowing the full amount allowed by LTV leaves less room for a price fall than borrowing below that ceiling.

Why not use a flash loan instead?

A Flash loan is an exception to the usual collateral requirement, but it is not an ordinary loan you can keep open. It lets a user borrow without putting up collateral only because the borrowed amount must be repaid within the same transaction. If repayment does not happen, the whole transaction reverts, as though it did not complete.

That makes a Flash loan useful for operations that can borrow and repay in one bundled transaction. It does not replace a collateralized loan for someone who wants to borrow DAI and hold it after the transaction ends. For that kind of borrowing, the protocol needs collateral that remains available to cover the debt.

What should you watch in your position?

Before borrowing, compare the amount you plan to take with the asset’s LTV, then consider how far the position sits from its Liquidation threshold. A loan below the LTV ceiling starts with more room for a price decline than one at the maximum. The amount of room changes as collateral prices and the debt’s value change.

For a new DeFi borrower, that is the key difference from a bank loan: income and credit history do not stand behind the debt in the same way. The supplied crypto does. LTV sets the borrowing ceiling, the higher Liquidation threshold marks where liquidation can begin, and the gap between them is the buffer. The signals to watch are the collateral’s price and the position’s distance from that threshold.
