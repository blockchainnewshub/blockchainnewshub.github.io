CFTC staff allow crypto in operating reserves, exclude it from liquidity minimums

CFTC staff say bitcoin, ether and payment stablecoins can count toward a year of operating costs, but not the separate cash and liquid securities minimum.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomNov 24, 2026 · 03:03 UTC#1448ad3 min read

Crypto can now count toward some derivatives venues’ operating reserves under CFTC staff guidance, but it cannot replace cash or highly liquid securities in separate liquidity buffers. The November 23 advisory clarifies how existing rules apply: eligible assets may help cover a year of costs, subject to haircuts, while venues must still meet their liquidity requirements with qualifying assets.

The CFTC’s announcement of the staff advisory covers swap execution facilities, designated contract markets and derivatives clearing organizations. The full advisory identifies bitcoin, ether and payment stablecoins as assets staff considers potentially acceptable for operating-cost requirements. That is a limited opening compared with relying only on conventional capital and other resources accepted under the rules: crypto may contribute to the reserve, but it does not satisfy every part of the firms’ financial-resource obligations.

One-year reserve, with reductions for risk

Across the three types of venue, the rules require financial resources sufficient to cover at least one year of operating costs. The advisory says bitcoin, ether and payment stablecoins may count toward that amount, with appropriate haircuts. It does not make those assets equivalent to cash: their value must be reduced to account for risk, so the amount credited toward a reserve is below their current market value.

For swap execution facilities and designated contract markets, staff said it would not object to minimum haircuts of 20% for bitcoin and ether and 2% for payment stablecoins. A crypto asset that is not readily marketable and is not a payment stablecoin would generally receive a 100% haircut, leaving it with no value for meeting the requirement. For clearing organizations, the advisory gives the same minimum haircut figures and says reductions must reflect market, credit and liquidity risks.

The figures show the trade-off: the guidance offers firms another potential source of reserve value, while discounting that value to reflect the risks of holding crypto. Staff also says venues should consider “wrong-way risk”—the possibility that operational losses and falls in crypto prices could occur together.

Liquidity floors remain in cash or securities

The reserve allowance does not carry over to the separate liquidity test. For swap execution facilities, at least three months of the one-year financial resources must be unencumbered cash or highly liquid securities, or cover projected wind-down costs if that amount is greater. Designated contract markets and clearing organizations must hold enough qualifying liquid assets to cover at least six months of operating costs. Crypto, including payment stablecoins, does not qualify as cash under GAAP or as highly liquid securities under the advisory.

That split means crypto can support the broader operating-cost buffer, while firms still need a substantial pool of assets the rules recognize for liquidity. A stablecoin may face a lower haircut than bitcoin or ether for the reserve calculation, but it still cannot fill the liquidity minimum. Firms therefore gain flexibility in one part of the calculation without being able to use crypto across the balance sheet.

The scope is also narrow. The advisory addresses the operating-resource provisions in Regulations 37.1301, 38.1101(a) and 39.11(a)(2); it expressly does not address whether crypto can meet a clearing organization’s default-resource requirement. And the guidance represents staff views only: it creates no binding rule or enforceable rights, is not a no-action position and may not represent the Commission’s view.

The next signals are how registered venues apply the specified haircuts and liquidity floors in practice, and whether staff addresses other crypto assets. The advisory directs firms seeking to use assets beyond bitcoin, ether and payment stablecoins to consult the relevant CFTC division.
