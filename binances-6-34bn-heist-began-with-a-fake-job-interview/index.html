Binance’s $6.34bn heist began with a fake job interview

Binance says a months-long staff compromise enabled its $6.34bn theft, eclipsing Bybit's $1.5bn loss; freezes and company funds limit user losses.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomNov 8, 2026 · 13:39 UTC#aec3343 min read

Binance says hackers stole $6.34 billion in a months-long operation that began with employee compromises, making the October heist more than four times the $1.5 billion Bybit lost in 2025. In its November 3 account, CoinDesk reported Binance’s briefing on the attack, which the exchange said involved a fake job interview, malicious software and an internal AI tool. The scale sets it apart from a conventional phishing incident: the attackers built access over months before reaching Binance’s wallet infrastructure.

How did the interview become an entry point?

Binance said the campaign’s first known foothold came in June, when one engineer installed a tampered Homebrew package while looking for an AI tool. The exchange detected the installation and replaced the device, but said the engineer’s role and information about colleagues on the wallet team had been exposed.

In September, a different engineer was approached on a legitimate job-hunting platform by someone claiming to be a project director. According to Binance, a fake human-resources representative conducted a video interview, followed by a second interview with a supposed chief executive. The engineer was then asked to download a coding assignment containing malware that evaded endpoint security.

On October 20, the attackers used the compromised machine and an internal AI tool to reach two wallet engineers’ computers, Binance said. Nine days later, they used stolen credentials from the first engineer on the second developer’s computer, deployed a container and ran a malicious script. Funds began leaving wallets at 20:05 UTC.

What made the wallet transfers possible?

Binance said the attackers exploited Ethereum’s EIP-7702 feature to establish execution rights over a hot wallet. That shifted the breach from compromised employee devices to authorization over wallet transactions, allowing transfers outside the exchange’s normal withdrawal process, according to the company’s account.

Binance’s hot wallets use multiparty computation, a setup in which no single key controls the wallet. That design did not provide a single switch to revoke the newly established permission: CoinDesk reported that Binance said containment took until 00:10 UTC on October 30. The trade-off exposed by the incident is specific. Distributed key control can prevent one key holder from acting alone, but it did not stop this attack once the attackers had gained access through compromised devices and credentials.

The sequence also puts the internal AI tool in context. Binance said it helped attackers reach engineers’ computers; its account does not establish that the tool itself was vulnerable. The interview and malicious software supplied the longer path into the company, while the tool formed part of the later access chain.

How much was recovered, and what comes next?

Binance said about $2.85 billion of the stolen assets had been frozen or intercepted and that it had replenished about 98% of the affected tokens with company funds, telling CoinDesk users would not be affected. Freezing can limit what attackers can move when assets are within reach of intermediaries that can act; it offers less direct control over assets that cannot be frozen by a third party. The amount intercepted is therefore a recovery measure, not proof that the rest has been recovered.

The FBI attributed the attack to North Korea-linked group TraderTraitor, according to the Associated Press report; North Korea denied involvement. Binance said it would bring in independent cybersecurity experts to review the incident and that it had blocked 57 suspected North Korea-linked attempts this year. The next signals are the independent review’s findings, whether more of the stolen assets can be frozen, and what Binance changes in its device, credential and wallet authorization controls.
