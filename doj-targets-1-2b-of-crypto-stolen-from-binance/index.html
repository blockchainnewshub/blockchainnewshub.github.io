DOJ targets $1.2B of crypto stolen from Binance

The DOJ has court authority to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of Binance's stolen crypto, while tracing continues across bridges, mixers and exchanges.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomNov 20, 2026 · 01:54 UTC#33f3033 min read

The U.S. Justice Department has court authority to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion stolen from Binance, a step toward legal control of funds after government agencies and private-sector partners froze more than $2.7 billion. The overall theft exceeded $6 billion, so the order covers a substantial but limited share of the stolen assets. The Justice Department’s announcement said the Oct. 29 attack was carried out by TraderTraitor, a group the FBI identifies as North Korean military-linked.

From frozen funds to court-authorized action

The distinction between freezing and seizure matters. A freeze can stop assets moving while investigators trace them; court authorization to seize or restrain gives the government a legal basis to take control. The DOJ has not said in its announcement that the funds have been forfeited or returned to Binance or affected users. The Associated Press reported that the government typically files a forfeiture claim with a judge, who then determines who has a legitimate claim to recovered assets. That process leaves a legal step between blocking the funds and deciding where they ultimately go.

The $1.2 billion is part of the more than $2.7 billion frozen through combined government and private-sector efforts, according to the DOJ. It is not an additional recovery on top of that amount. The scale also sets a clear limit on what the action establishes: the order gives authorities a route to pursue a portion of the theft, while the remaining stolen funds are still subject to tracing and possible intervention.

Tracing continues as funds move

The DOJ said TraderTraitor actors are laundering the assets through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. Those routes can move value between networks or make the connection between a theft and later wallets harder to follow. The department said its efforts to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit the assets are ongoing; it also cited an FBI alert urging virtual-asset service providers to block transactions with, or derived from, addresses used in the laundering.

This makes cooperation from exchanges and other service providers part of the recovery effort. A court order can support government action, but tracing still has to identify funds as they move and providers have to act before assets pass beyond their reach. The DOJ credited rapid work with industry partners for freezing billions, while its own statement said the laundering effort is continuing. Those two facts mark both the progress and the constraint: a large amount has been stopped, but the work is not complete.

A separate campaign, with a different mechanism

The same Nov. 12 announcement covered a separate North Korean operation called FaceStealer. The FBI says it stole more than $400 million in cryptocurrency over the previous 18 months. According to the DOJ, attackers used invitations to video meetings, recordings of people known to their targets and deepfake videos to build trust, then presented a fake technical problem that prompted victims to install malware. The FBI seized more than 900 webcam videos under a warrant.

FaceStealer is not identified as the method behind the Binance theft. The two cases show different enforcement targets: cloud infrastructure and evidence in one social-engineering scheme, and court-authorized control of assets tied to the Binance breach. The next signals are whether investigators and service providers can trace and freeze more of the stolen funds, whether the authorized assets move into forfeiture proceedings, and how much can ultimately be returned to legitimate claimants.
