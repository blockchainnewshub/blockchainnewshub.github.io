US courts authorize seizure of $1.2bn in Binance hack

The Justice Department says courts have authorized seizure or restraint of more than $1.2 billion from Binance’s $6.3 billion hack, while tracing continues.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomNov 13, 2026 · 11:55 UTC#c6e4483 min read

U.S. authorities have won court authorization to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, a step that puts a legal hold on part of the proceeds while leaving most of the reported $6.3 billion theft outside that authorization. The Associated Press report says the FBI announced the seizure on Thursday; the Justice Department describes the amount as court-authorized seizure or restraint, within a larger pool of funds frozen by government and private-sector partners.

The difference between those figures matters. More than $2.7 billion of the stolen assets has been frozen so far, according to the Justice Department, but that is a combined total from U.S. government and private-sector action. The more than $1.2 billion is the portion for which the department says it has obtained court authorization. Neither figure means that all of the money has been returned to Binance or its customers.

From the October theft to a court-backed hold

The Justice Department says members of the North Korean military, referred to by the FBI as TraderTraitor, stole more than $6 billion in virtual assets from Binance on or about October 29. The AP puts the reported loss at about $6.3 billion and describes it as the largest crypto heist on record. That places the newly authorized amount at less than one-fifth of the reported loss, while the wider frozen total is somewhat under half.

Since the theft, U.S. agencies have worked with virtual asset service providers to identify and interdict the funds. The Justice Department’s announcement says investigators are still tracing, freezing, seizing and pursuing forfeiture of the assets. It also says TraderTraitor actors continue to move funds through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. The FBI had issued an alert the previous week asking virtual asset providers to block transactions involving addresses used to launder the stolen Binance assets.

The response relies on a mix of cooperation and legal authority. Private firms and service providers can help freeze assets within systems they control; court authorization gives the government a basis to pursue seizure or restraint through the legal process. The fast freezes have limited how much of the reported haul moved beyond immediate reach, but the remaining tracing and forfeiture work shows the limits of that intervention: moving assets across services can complicate recovery, and a freeze is not the same as a final transfer to victims.

Authorization is not the final recovery

The court-backed action gives authorities a stronger legal position over the covered funds, but the AP notes that the government typically files a forfeiture claim with a judge, who then determines who has a legitimate claim to recovered assets. The announcement does not say that the $1.2 billion has been returned, identify its specific tokens or provide a timetable for any forfeiture case.

The next signals are whether authorities can trace and freeze more of the remaining funds, whether the authorized amount proceeds through forfeiture, and how much is ultimately returned. For now, the $2.7 billion figure measures funds frozen across public and private efforts; the $1.2 billion figure marks a court-authorized step within that total. The gap between those milestones and a completed recovery will show how much of the reported loss can be brought back under lasting legal control.
