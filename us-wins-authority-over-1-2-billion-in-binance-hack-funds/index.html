US wins authority over $1.2 billion in Binance hack funds

The Justice Department has court authority to seize or restrain $1.2 billion of Binance hack funds, while government and industry partners have frozen more than $2.7 billion.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomNov 15, 2026 · 19:06 UTC#fc09833 min read

The U.S. Justice Department has court authority to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion in cryptocurrency stolen from Binance, a step beyond the broader freeze of more than $2.7 billion already imposed by government and private-sector partners. The Justice Department’s announcement on Nov. 12 puts a legal process behind part of the recovery effort, but it does not say the funds have been forfeited or returned to victims.

The distinction matters. Freezing assets can stop them moving while investigators trace them; court-authorized seizure or restraint gives the government a route to pursue control of specific funds. Forfeiture is a further legal step: as the Associated Press reported, the government typically files a forfeiture claim, after which a judge determines who has a legitimate claim to recovered assets.

More than $2.7 billion frozen after the October theft

The FBI attributes the Binance theft to members of the North Korean military it calls TraderTraitor. The Justice Department said the theft took place on or about Oct. 29 and involved more than $6 billion in virtual assets. AP described the amount as about $6.3 billion and called it the largest crypto heist in history.

Those figures show both the scale of the response and its limits. The combined freeze of more than $2.7 billion is a significant portion of the reported loss, but it is not the same as recovering that amount for Binance or its customers. Nor does the court authorization cover the entire frozen pool: the department says it has authority to seize or restrain more than $1.2 billion of the frozen funds.

The response depends on coordination with virtual asset service providers, according to the Justice Department. That offers investigators and firms a way to block funds as they move, while the legal process can establish who may ultimately claim them. The trade-off is that a freeze can interrupt transfers quickly, but does not itself resolve ownership or guarantee that assets will be returned. The department says the work to trace, freeze, seize and forfeit the funds remains underway.

Investigators say laundering efforts continue

The FBI said last week it had issued a cyber alert attributing the theft and urging virtual asset service providers to block transactions involving addresses used by TraderTraitor, including funds derived from those addresses. The Justice Department says the actors continue to launder assets through cryptocurrency bridges, mixers and exchanges. That makes continued tracing and cooperation with service providers central to whether more of the stolen funds can be intercepted.

The seizure announcement also came alongside a separate action involving FaceStealer, a North Korean social engineering scheme. The Justice Department says it disrupted cloud infrastructure used in the scheme, which it says stole more than $400 million in cryptocurrency over the last 18 months. That operation is separate from the Binance theft; the two cases do not establish a shared method or a connection between the victims.

The next signals are legal and operational: whether the government files forfeiture claims for the newly authorized assets, whether judges recognize claims to them, and whether investigators and service providers can freeze more funds as the laundering trail develops. Until those steps are reported, the $1.2 billion figure describes court-authorized seizure or restraint, not money confirmed returned.
