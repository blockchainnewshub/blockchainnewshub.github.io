Why a Bridge Needs ERC-20 Approval Before Depositing

Why does bridging need an approval transaction first? An ERC-20 bridge uses transferFrom, which needs an allowance set with approve before it can record your deposit.

By Crypto Docket NewsroomNov 25, 2026 · 10:09 UTC#ca5cff4 min read

After you choose to bridge 500 USDC from Arbitrum, the first transaction approves the bridge to use those tokens; the second asks it to send them across. That extra step comes from how ERC-20 handles permissions: the bridge contract must pull the tokens with transferFrom, and your approve transaction sets the allowance that permits the pull. cBridge follows this pattern for ERC-20 deposits. Its cbridge refund topic adds useful context about what can happen after a transfer when a refund is needed; cBridge’s deposit flow starts with the separate allowance step.

Approval gives the bridge permission to pull your tokens

An ERC-20 approval sets an allowance that lets a named spender move tokens from your account. EIP-20 defines the functions approve, allowance and transferFrom for this permission model. The bridge contract is the spender in this case.

In the Arbitrum case, transaction one calls approve(cBridge contract, 500) on the USDC token contract. That records an allowance of 500 USDC for the cBridge contract. The approval does not itself move the USDC or start the cross-chain transfer; it establishes the permission the next transaction will use.

Transaction two calls the bridge’s send() function. As part of handling that request, the bridge calls transferFrom on USDC to pull the 500 tokens from your account. The token contract checks the allowance. EIP-20 requires the transfer to revert if the requested amount exceeds it, so without sufficient permission the bridge cannot complete that pull.

A plain token transfer cannot tell the bridge where to send funds

An ERC-20 transfer() moves tokens to an address without calling the recipient contract. The token contract updates balances, but the receiving bridge contract does not get a callback that tells it a deposit just arrived.

That leaves the bridge without the information it needs to process a cross-chain request. A transfer to the bridge’s address does not, by itself, identify the depositor’s intended destination chain or receiver. The tokens could arrive at the contract without a corresponding instruction for where the bridge should pay them out.

The bridge’s send() call joins the instruction and the token movement. It receives the destination chain and receiver as part of the request, then calls transferFrom to pull the tokens under the allowance. The bridge can record the deposit alongside those destination details in the same transaction. The approval comes first because the contract needs permission before it can make that pull.

The second transaction combines the deposit with its destination

In the 500 USDC case, the first transaction records who may pull the tokens and how much. The second tells the bridge what to do with them. When send() calls transferFrom, the bridge pulls the USDC and logs the destination chain and receiver associated with the request.

That separation reflects two different jobs. The token contract manages permission over the user’s balance; the bridge contract manages the cross-chain instruction. The approval is addressed to the token contract and names the bridge as spender. The send request is addressed to the bridge and carries the details it needs to handle the deposit.

This is the practical trade-off of the ERC-20 allowance model: users make a separate permission transaction, while the bridge can pull tokens as part of a request that includes destination information. A direct transfer would be one token movement, but it would not give the bridge the same instruction to associate with the deposit.

Native ETH needs no approval because it travels with the call

Native ETH skips the approval transaction because the user can attach the ETH value to the bridge’s send() call as msg.value. The value arrives with the request itself, so there is no separate ERC-20 token contract allowance for the bridge to obtain.

That is why bridging ETH can take one transaction while bridging an ERC-20 token typically takes two: the ETH and its destination instruction arrive together, while an ERC-20 bridge must first receive permission to pull tokens and then process the request that specifies where they should go.
